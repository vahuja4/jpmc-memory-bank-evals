Memory Bank Rescored

Memory Bank Rescored

Seven experiments on Google's Memory Bank, used as the memory behind a bank's customer-service assistant. Every result is re-read under the rules Google gave us on 29 September for what the product should and should not store. For each experiment you see what went in, what a right answer needed, and what came out.

Experiments 23 to 29 September 2026Generated customers only, no real customer dataAnswers graded by a stronger AI model, spot-checked by hand

The yardstick

What Memory Bank is meant to store, and what we score it on

Google looked at our first results page and told us three things about how the product is meant to be used. They change which questions are fair to ask, so they come first.

1. What goes into memory

Stored: what the customer said, what the bank promised to do (written as "Agent action: ..."), and advice the customer agreed to.

Not stored, on purpose: the agent's explanations of fees, rates and policy. If a question asks for one of those, memory is not expected to answer it, and we do not count it as a miss.

2. What stays in the bank's records

Identifiers, contact details, card and dispute status, amounts and case numbers belong on a records screen that the assistant always sees. They are not memory's job.

If the records screen answers a question, it is not a memory test. We do not score those here.

3. How to feed and read it

Conversations are sent one turn at a time, each marked as customer or bank, not as one block of text. A briefing before a call gets every note, not just the closest few. Every score is shown next to the best it could possibly be.

Under these rules, the main experiment's 165 questions split three ways. 60 ask for something an agent explained, so memory is not expected to know them. 64 are answered by the records screen. The other 41 are what memory is for: promises and whether they were kept, things the customer only thinks they asked for, what happened on a call that dropped, and what an agent needs to know before a contact. Every memory score on this page is on those 41 unless it says otherwise.

Vocabulary

The four ways of remembering used on this page

Records screen

The bank's own account records for the customer: cards, disputes, contact details, fees. The assistant sees them for every answer in experiment 5.

Raw transcripts

No AI when storing. Conversations are saved word for word and cut into pages. For each question, the 8 pages closest to it are handed over.

Memory Bank notes

An AI reads each conversation and writes short notes. Notes on the same subject are merged into one. "Default topics" means Google's standard setup. "Custom topics" means the three topics we wrote ourselves.

Whole history

The assistant is given everything, every time. The most accurate, and by far the most expensive.

Part one

Search and note-writing, checked on known answers

1. Search and note-writing on a small hand-labelled set

What it means

The search finds the right memory even when the question is vague, but it cannot tell a strong match from a weak one. The right memory and unrelated ones come back almost equally close. So you cannot set a bar and drop everything below it. Experiment 2 shows what happens when you try.

The notes are safe and keep what the customer said. Of the 9 things memory should have kept, 8 are in the notes and 1 was dropped. The exact values the customer gave (digits, amounts, error codes) were dropped too. Under Google's rules those belong in the records, not in memory.

2. Does the search put the right record in front of the assistant?

23 September, 36 generated customers, about 10 records each
Summary

Each of 36 customers has a history in which one record explains the problem they are about to call about. We wrote the records straight into Memory Bank, with no note-writing, so we know the right answer. Then we asked the question four ways: with no memory, with a strict similarity bar, with Memory Bank's search, and with every record in the prompt.

Reframed. Google says to keep the bank's records in the prompt and use Memory Bank only for conversations. So this experiment tests the search, not a setup anyone should use. What it shows about the search still holds.

What it means

A vague question and a plain similarity search do not make a good shortlist. To the search, "why is nothing working?" sounds about as close to a statement notice as to a fraud alert. So which records make the cut is close to luck, and the more routine records there are, the worse the odds. Having a model rewrite the question first made no difference. Anything smarter is for the bank to build. Google's advice to keep records out of memory removes most of the problem anyway.

Part two

What the note-writer keeps and drops

4. Long, messy messages

24 September, pilot on 2 customers, 5 messages, 28 planted facts, 17 questions
Summary

Long emails, chats and branch write-ups of the kind customers and staff really produce: pasted quotes of old messages, corrections, a spouse writing for the customer, long disclaimers. Each one hides planted facts and traps. We checked whether each fact was stored, and whether a question about it was answered.

Read with care. Every planted fact here is an exact value: a case number, an amount, a date, a phone number, an email, a merchant, a city, an error code. Under Google's rules those belong in the records, not in memory. So these numbers show how the note-writer handles values, not how well memory does its own job. The safety findings stand.

What it means

Stored as written, nothing is lost. Written up by the AI, the gist stays and the value goes. The note-writer turns a new phone number or email into "the customer updated their contact details". That is fine for a profile and useless for a callback. It fits Google's rule that values live in the records, and it means the bank has to write them there itself.

One mix-up of who is who appeared here first. The customer said her wife's card ending 8616 "works fine, this charge isn't on her card". The merged profile note made the wife an authorised user on the customer's account with that card. Experiment 6 looks at this kind of error properly.

Part three

The 41 questions memory is for

The main experiment. Five customers, each with 12 long conversations by phone, chat, email and branch over six months, plus 30 routine records. The conversations were sent to Memory Bank one turn at a time, marked as customer or bank, on two setups: Google's default topics and our custom topics. A third setup also added the bank's promises as separate notes, in the form Google suggested. For every answer, the assistant also had the records screen.

5. Six months of conversations, scored on what memory is for

Rerun on 29 September, turns marked by speaker. 5 customers, 60 conversations, 41 questions that only memory can answer.
Which questions

We wrote 165 questions for these customers. 60 ask for something an agent explained (fees, rates, policy), which memory is not meant to store. 64 are answered by the records screen. That leaves 41:

  • Was a promise kept, 10. "A supervisor was supposed to ring me about the dispute. I'm still waiting for that call."
  • Never happened, 10. "I'm pretty sure I asked to have my credit limit decreased a while back." The right answer is that there is no record.
  • Briefing before a contact, 13. "What should I know about this customer: open issues, promises made, recent changes?"
  • A call that dropped, 5. "I was on the phone about my new card and we got cut off. When was it actually switched on?"
  • Asked sideways, 3. "I called a few weeks ago because my payment wasn't on time. Could you offer the same kind of help?"

What it means

On the questions memory is for, notes beside the records get 34 of 41. Reading everything gets 39, at ten times the reading. Raw transcripts also get 34 but read more than twice as much.

What is kept from the bank's side depends on the topics you set up, not on marking who spoke. Default topics drop the agent's explanations, as Google intends. But they also drop what the customer later says about whether a promise was kept, and that costs 6 of the 10 promise questions. Custom topics keep both. The same thing shows in the 60 agent-explanation questions: default topics answer 3, custom topics 57.

Adding the bank's promises as separate notes did not help on custom topics, because those topics already captured them. Google's recommended pairing, default topics plus logged promises, has not been run yet.

Twice the memory held the answer and the assistant still did not use it. With a note saying a statement copy had been promised, one answer said "we do not have information confirming if it has been sent or received". Having the records screen in the prompt makes the assistant cautious about anything not on it.

5b. How the rerun was built, and what it stored

29 September, run 242f33. About an hour: 15 memory builds, then 1,155 answers graded.
What went in

Each of the 60 conversations was split into turns before it was sent. Lines from the customer, or from someone on the customer's side such as a brother or a spouse, were sent as the customer. Lines from the agent, the phone menu, the virtual assistant and the bank's emails were sent as the bank. A branch write-up has no turns, so it was sent as one entry from the bank's side. The 30 routine notes per customer (statements, autopay, alerts) were not sent at all. They are bank records, not conversation.

What it means

Feeding conversations the way Google asked works as described. Sent turn by turn on default topics, Memory Bank keeps what the customer said and the bank's promises, drops the agent's explanations, and writes in the customer's voice.

What default topics do not keep is the customer's own report that a promise was kept. "They actually did call" was never stored on the default setup. That is why default topics score 4 of 10 on promise questions and custom topics 9 of 10.

Logging promises ourselves did not help on custom topics. It has not yet been tried on default topics, which is the pairing Google recommends.

Part four

Are the notes true?

6 and 7. Every note read against its source, then questions aimed at the wrong ones

26 and 29 September, five customers, every flag checked by hand
What was done

A bigger model read every note Memory Bank wrote against the conversations it came from and flagged the notes that state something false. Every flag was checked by hand. Then the assistant was asked questions a customer might ask about those points, to see whether the false fact reached them.

What was found

Fed the way Google asked, about one note in nine is wrong with custom topics and one in sixty with default topics, down from one in sixteen the old way. Almost every wrong note is something a person really said, the customer's guess or an agent's mistake, written down as fact. When a customer asks about that point, the assistant repeats it: in 17 of 27 answers with custom topics, 6 of 27 with default topics. Reading the whole transcript does no better, because the false sentence is in the transcript too.

What it means

Memory Bank records what it is told and does not check it. That is by design. A wrong note looks exactly like a right one, and the bank's records only catch the cases they happen to cover. An external, domain-specific validation layer is needed: something that checks each note against the bank's records and the source conversation before it is trusted, and that can store a customer's claim as a claim rather than as a fact.

Outside check
"Assistant's statement never stored" is now labelled as the product working as designed.
  1. The search is reliable on vague customer wording, but it cannot tell what matters. The right memory was in the top 5 for 14 of 14 questions. But a fraud alert and a statement notice look equally close to "why is nothing working?". There is no usable similarity bar, no preference for recent records and no second look at the results. Anything smarter is for the bank to build, and keeping records out of memory removes most of the need.
  2. On the 41 questions memory is for, notes beside the records get 34. Reading everything gets 39 at ten times the reading. Raw transcripts also get 34 at more than twice the reading.
  3. The topics you set up decide what is kept from the bank's side, not marking who spoke. Google's default topics drop the agent's explanations, as intended, and also drop the customer's report that a promise was kept. That costs 6 of 10 promise questions. Custom topics keep it. The pairing Google recommends, default topics plus logged promises, is the next run.
  4. The note-writer keeps the gist and drops the value. "I changed my number" survives; the digits do not. Under Google's design that is fine, because values live in the records. It means the bank must write them there itself.
  5. Fed the new way, expect about one wrong note per customer with our topics, and hardly any with Google's defaults. It was two or three per customer the old way. What remains is mostly the customer's own guess stored as fact, plus a few merge mistakes. Where a record covers the point, the assistant followed the record. Where the records are silent, the wrong note reaches the customer: 17 of 27 answers with our topics, 6 of 27 with the defaults. What is missing is a way to mark a note as "this is what the customer claimed", and the product has none.
  6. Marking who spoke does not by itself keep the bank's words out. With Google's defaults the bank's side is left out, and the agent's mistakes with it. With our advice topic the bank's lines are stored, wrong ones included: 2 of the 8 remaining errors are an agent's wrong claim. Anything the bank wants remembered from its own side, a promise or a courtesy, has to be asked for on purpose, and that is also the door a wrong agent claim comes through.

Still open. Google's recommended pairing (default topics plus logged promises) has not been run. The 41 questions lean on promises and things that never happened. A set about lasting customer context (preferences, family, travel, how they want to be contacted, their own account of events) is the fair next test, and is our proposal for a run done together with Google. Ten benchmark questions should become more before any score is quoted.
