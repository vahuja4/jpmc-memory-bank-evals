Eight experiments on giving a bank's assistant a memory

Over six days we tested Google's Memory Bank, a service that remembers customers between conversations, against simpler alternatives. This page summarises each experiment in plain terms, shows its results, and says what we concluded.

23 to 28 September 2026Generated customers only, no real customer dataAnswers graded by a stronger AI model, spot-checked by hand
The question
When a customer contacts the bank, the assistant should already know what they were told last time. Google's Memory Bank offers to build that memory automatically: an AI reads each conversation and writes notes for later. Is that better than simpler options, and can the notes be trusted?
The short answer
Memory of some kind is essential: without it the assistant got the customer's problem right 1 time in 9. On long, messy conversations, Memory Bank's merged notes were the best practical memory, within two answers of re-reading everything at a twentieth of the reading. The notes carry two or three errors per customer, mostly the customer's own misstatements, and those rarely change an answer.
Where it stands
The service's search is solid. Its weak spots are briefings that need a wide view of the account, facts the agent said rather than the customer, and dates. There is no setting that stops a customer's claim being stored as fact.

Vocabulary

The four ways of remembering that appear throughout

Search the raw transcripts

No AI at storage time. Conversations are saved word for word, cut into pages. For each question the 8 most relevant pages are looked up.

AI-written notes

Memory Bank, plain mode. An AI reads each conversation and writes short factual notes. The 8 most relevant notes are looked up.

Memory Bank with merging on. Notes about the same subject are combined into one running note that is rewritten as things change.

Whole history, no search

The assistant is handed everything, every time. The ceiling on accuracy and by far the most expensive.

Part one

Does memory matter at all?

1. Search and note quality on a small hand-labelled set

23 September
Summary

Before anything else we checked the two halves of the service on 14 questions with known answers. Does its search find the right memory? And when it writes its own notes from a conversation, does it capture what a bank would need, and nothing it should not? The notes were checked against a list of the facts a bank would need from each conversation and a list of things that must never be stored.

Snapshot
A conversation the AI had to take notes from
CUSTOMER: I'm in London right now and Apple Pay won't let me add my Sapphire card. It says CARD_STATUS_LOCKED_RESTRICTED. I set a travel notice before I left, reference trv-lon-2026.
CUSTOMER: This is on my iPhone 16 Pro. Also the airline food on the way over was awful, lol. Can you just unlock it?
Had to capture: the travel notice trv-lon-2026 is active for London. Must not store: the airline food complaint.
A search question and the memory it had to find

"overseas payment did not go through....why?"

On Day 2 at 11:20 UTC, while in London, the customer's attempt to add card *4821 to Apple Pay on an iPhone 16 Pro failed with error CARD_STATUS_LOCKED_RESTRICTED.
This memory came back second, just behind an unrelated declined purchase. The security lock and the travel notice, which explain the failure, were not in the top 5. Asked directly as "Why did Apple Pay fail while I was in London?", all three came back in the top 5 with this one first. This was the worst of the 14 rewordings.

Conclusion

The search finds the right memory even when the customer asks vaguely. It was always in the top 5 and usually first. But the search is much less sure of itself. When the question was direct, the right memory stood out clearly from the rest. When the question was vague, the right memory only just edged out memories that had nothing to do with it. That means the search cannot tell a good match from a poor one, so you cannot set a bar and drop everything below it. The next experiment shows what happens when you try. This was also a small test, 20 memories for one customer. Real accounts are bigger, so expect worse.

The AI's notes are safe but leave a lot out. It never wrote down a card number and never made anything up. But it captured only 2 in every 5 facts the bank would need later. It writes down what the customer says about themselves and skips what the agent or the bank's systems said.

2. Does the assistant's answer actually depend on memory?

23 September, 36 generated customers
Summary

We generated 36 customers, each with a history that explains a problem they are about to ask about. Then we asked the question with different amounts of memory available and checked whether the assistant named the real cause.

Snapshot
The record in the customer's history that explains her problem
FRAUD VELOCITY & STEP-UP ALERT: Concurrent session logins detected from Phoenix, AZ and Miami, FL within 7 minutes, accompanied by a $640.00 POS charge at Northgate Camera & Audio in Miami, FL […]. Automated containment placed Chase Ink Business Cash (*1791) on SECURITY_LOCKED restriction.
One of 10 records for this customer. The other nine are older or unrelated: a past trip, a resolved fraud case, a mortgage enquiry.
What the customer asked

"I've had three declines in two days. Why?"

Counted as right if the answer traced the declines back to the security lock from the fraud alert, which was never lifted because the verification call was cut off.
Results
Conclusion

Memory is the difference between 11% and 97%. Any reasonable memory works here, whether Memory Bank's search or reading the whole history. One thing to avoid: a strict cutoff on how similar a memory must be to the question. Customers ask vaguely, so the cutoff discarded the memories that mattered and accuracy fell to 22%.

The 97% comes with a caveat: each customer here has only about 10 records. The next experiment keeps everything else the same and makes the history longer, and the score drops to 50%.

3. What happens when the history gets long

23 September, 36 customers with 30 records each
Summary

Real customers have years of routine records. So we took the same 36 customers and the same questions, and grew each history from about 10 records to 30 by adding routine ones: statements, balance checks, payments. The record that explains the problem is now the oldest of the 30.

The assistant never sees the whole history. Memory Bank picks the records that look most like the question and hands over only those. We ran it two ways: handing over the 8 closest records, and handing over the 16 closest.

Snapshot
The kind of routine record used as padding
STATEMENT READY: [card] statement posted (balance [amount]; minimum payment [amount]; due date [date]). No fees or interest charged this cycle.
Twenty of these, with real-looking values filled in, were added to the customer above, alongside her 10 real records. Balance checks and payment confirmations were used the same way.
Same question, longer history

"I've had three declines in two days. Why?"

With 8 records handed over, the fraud alert was not among them. The answer implied the trouble started with the failed verification call and never mentioned the alert that caused the lock. With 16 records it was there.
Results
Conclusion

A vague question and a plain similarity search do not make a good shortlist. The search picks the records that sound most like the question. "Why is nothing working?" does not sound like a fraud alert, and it does not sound like a statement notice either. To the search they are all about equally close. So which records make the cut is close to luck, and the more routine records there are, the worse the odds for the one that matters. With 8 handed over, the fraud alert made the shortlist for 65% of customers. With 16, for 90%, which still leaves 1 customer in 10 with the key record missing. Every miss was an incomplete answer, never a wrong one. Setting a similarity bar and keeping only records above it does not help, because the important record and the routine ones score almost the same.

This is worth understanding about the service. Its search is simple by design. It turns the question into a numerical fingerprint, compares that with every stored memory, and returns the closest ones. The only settings are how many to return and, optionally, a similarity bar. It does not favour recent records, does not know that a fraud alert matters more than a statement notice, does not take a second look at the results, and does not rewrite a vague question into a better one. Anything smarter than that is for the bank to build on top. We tried the one cheap improvement, having a model rephrase the question before the search, and it made no difference.

Part two

Is the AI note-writer worth it?

In part one, the records were written by us and stored word for word. Memory Bank only searched them. The one exception was the six-conversation check in experiment 1, where the service's AI wrote the notes and kept 39% of what mattered. Having an AI write and merge the notes is the service's distinctive feature, and it costs time and money on every conversation. So two experiments asked whether it pays off.

4. Long, messy messages

24 September, pilot on 2 customers, 89 messages generated
Summary

We generated long emails and chat threads of the kind customers really send: pasted quotes of old messages, corrections, a spouse writing on the customer's behalf, long disclaimers. Each hides a few planted facts. We checked whether each fact was stored at all, and whether a question about it was answered.

Snapshot
Part of a branch visit write-up, in the banker's words
[…] I made a clumsy typo and entered the payment as $3,350.00. I was moving through the screens and just before I hit the final confirmation, I glanced down at the physical check on my desk to double-check. That's when I saw my mistake. […] I voided the initial entry and started over, this time very deliberately keying in the correct amount of $3,530.00. I turned my monitor so she could see and had her confirm the amount […]
Planted fact: the payment was $3,530.00. The mistyped $3,350.00 is a trap. The same write-up also has the banker promising same-day availability and then taking it back when a hold is placed, wrapped in small talk about a baseball team.
What was asked afterwards

"How much did the customer pay toward the card at the branch?"

Questions in this experiment were asked from the agent's side. All three approaches stored the right amount and answered correctly. The merged note for the same visit also recorded the receipt number, yet the question about that number was answered wrongly: stored is not the same as found.
Results
Conclusion

Stored as written, nothing is lost. Written up by the AI, one fact in five is. The facts it dropped were ones the customer had asked the bank to act on: a new callback number, a changed email address, the details of a dispute. Those are things a bank must keep. They are not the same as the things it must never keep, such as full card numbers and passwords, and the AI got that distinction right every time. The note-writer simply does not treat a new phone number as worth writing down.

Two smaller problems appeared here for the first time. The AI recorded a spouse's card as if it were the customer's own, and it kept some small talk. Both point to the same weakness: the notes do not track who said what, which experiment 6 looks at properly.

5. Six months of conversations per customer

25 to 26 September, 5 customers, 165 questions
Summary

The main experiment. Each of five customers has 12 long, messy conversations across phone, chat, email and branch over six months, plus 30 routine records. The questions are asked the way customers ask them, 30 or more days after the fact, and most test something an agent said once. An answer counts only if every required fact is present and nothing out of date is presented as current.

One of 12 conversations for this customer. The fee is mentioned once, in passing.

"i'm traveling internationally next week. am i going to get hit with big charges if i use my card to get local money from a machine over there?"

Counted as right if the answer gave both the $15.00 fee and the interest from day one at 31.49%.

Conclusion

Merged notes are the practical winner. They got 157 of 165 right, two behind reading the whole history, while reading a twentieth as much text.

Plain AI notes barely beat the transcripts. 142 against 139. When several dated notes cover the same subject, the assistant sometimes picks an old one. Merging them into one running note fixes that.

Merging loses some history. Of 33 details that changed over the six months, 12 old values can no longer be seen in the merged notes. Fine for "what is true now", a problem if a dispute ever needs the paper trail.

Briefings are the weak spot for every approach that hands over 8 items. A briefing needs the whole picture, and 8 items is not the whole picture. Only reading the full history got it right.

Part three

Can the notes be trusted?

6. Reading every stored note against its source

26 September, 363 notes from the five customers
Summary

A stronger model compared every note Memory Bank had written for the five customers with the conversations it came from. Before it started, it was given five kinds of error to look for, chosen because the worry was whether the notes keep track of who said what:

  • A person's wrong statement written down as fact.
  • Someone else's detail filed under the customer, such as a spouse's card.
  • A plan or a what-if written up as if it happened.
  • The wrong person quoted: the agent for the customer, or the reverse.
  • A plain mistake that nobody said.

Every note it flagged was then checked by hand against the conversation. The cases below are four of the confirmed ones, chosen to show each way a note goes wrong.

Results

What actually happenedphone call, 9 March, the dispute is opened

AGENT: Pinecrest Outdoor Supply. Okay, I see it now. It posted two days ago. […] So just to confirm, you did not authorize this charge from Pinecrest Outdoor Supply?

This is the customer's only dispute. No hotel charge was ever disputed or mentioned.

What was said latersecure chat, 29 June
CUSTOMER: I'm actually still waiting on a written summary of that dispute I filed. The one about the hotel charge.

The written summary of the customer's hotel charge dispute, originally promised in April 2026 within ten business days, was never sent; on 2026-06-29 agent Omar escalated the issue to his supervisor and the disputes department […]
Why it is wrong

In June the customer simply got the shop wrong. Memory Bank already held notes from March naming Pinecrest Outdoor Supply, but the June note did not check against them. It wrote down "hotel charge dispute" as if that were the name of the case, with nothing to show it was the customer's word.

CUSTOMER: You know, I think it was. Yes. It came. It took a while, but it got here.

In June she was guessing, and she guessed wrong. The note wrote "I think it was" down as "was successfully received". The July call put the record straight, but the June note stayed as written.

What it causedexperiment 8, with the bank's records on screen
CUSTOMER: hi, sorting some old papers for my accountant. can you just check for me if that statement copy was posted out?

ASSISTANT: We can confirm that the itemized paper copy of your March billing statement, related to dispute DSP-1512689, was successfully received. […]

She asked whether it was posted out. The assistant answered that it was received, which is the note's word, not hers, and a stronger claim than the question. In truth it was neither sent nor received. The bank's records had no trace of a copy going out, but they did not say it had not, so the assistant went with the note. This was one of the two questions out of ten where a wrong note beat the records.

3 Merging turns a cancelled plan into something that happened
What was saidphone call, 15 August
CUSTOMER: my friend and I were supposed to be going to Colombia in a couple of weeks but the whole trip has been postponed indefinitely due to a family issue on her end. So I need to take that travel notification off my card.
What Memory Bank storedmerged note about the customer
[…] She is planning a trip to Nigeria to visit her parents […] She traveled to Cartagena, Colombia, from 2026-08-16 to 2026-08-23 to attend a wedding. On 2026-08-31, she confirmed that her address on file in San Diego is current.
Why it is wrong

Nobody said she went. An earlier note recorded the travel notice for the trip. When the cancellation was merged in, the running note kept the trip and lost the cancellation.

4 Merging moves an old dispute onto the new card
What was saidphone call, 4 March, opening the dispute
AGENT: And just to be sure I am looking at the correct card, it's the one ending in three eight one zero?
CUSTOMER: Yes.
What Memory Bank storednote written after the July call that closed the dispute
As of 2026-07-24, dispute DSP-1480747 regarding a $313.89 charge from Harbor Lights Florist on the Amazon Prime card *1337 was officially closed in the customer's favor […]
Why it is wrong

Card *3810 was replaced by *1337 in June. The July note attached the March dispute to whichever card was current. Nobody said that, and it is exactly the kind of detail a records check would catch.

Across all 363 notes for the five customers, 23 had a confirmed error: 13 of the 274 plain notes and 10 of the 89 merged ones, about 2 or 3 per customer either way. 11 were a person's wrong statement written as fact, like cases 1 and 2. 12 were Memory Bank's own mistake, like cases 3 and 4, and most of those happened during merging. None filed someone else's details under the customer, stored a what-if as real, or quoted the wrong person.

Conclusion

Two or three wrong notes per customer, from two different causes. In plain notes, the main cause is the note-writer believing the customer: it records a guess or a misremembering as fact, with nothing to show it was the customer's word. In merged notes, the main cause is the merge itself: combining several notes about one subject is where disputes jump cards and cancelled trips become taken.

What Google gives you to deal with this.

  • You can write the descriptions and examples that tell the note-writer what to look for. This steers it. It does not let you tell it "treat what the customer says as a claim".
  • You can write the notes yourself and hand them to the service, checking them against the bank's records first. The service still merges them.
  • Every edit to a note is kept, and a note can be rolled back to an earlier version.
  • Merging can be turned down or off: only merge notes that share a tag such as a card or case number, keep more old versions, or store every note separately.

What it does not give you.

  • No way to mark a note as "claimed" rather than "verified". A note has no field for who said it or how sure the service is.
  • No way to change the instructions the note-writer actually runs on.
  • No hook to check a note against the bank's records before it is written. That check, which the records experiment below shows would catch most of these, has to be built by the bank.

7. Do the wrong notes change what the customer is told?

26 September, 8 questions, 3 repeats each, per approach
Summary

A wrong note only matters if it reaches an answer. We wrote eight customer questions aimed squarely at the confirmed errors and asked each three times under every approach, then checked the answers by hand.

Snapshot
What the customer asked

"When I changed my email back in March it took two goes because your chat agent got it wrong. I'd like that noted as a complaint about the agent."

In the March chat the customer typed the address with a missing dot, blamed a sticky keyboard, and corrected it. The agent did nothing wrong.

Results
Conclusion

Most wrong notes never reach the answer. A correct note is usually retrieved alongside the wrong one and the assistant follows it. The one harm that survived everywhere, even with the whole history, is a customer who blamed the agent for her own email typo: every approach repeated the blame. That is partly the assistant believing the customer, not only memory.

Part four

Memory next to the bank's own records

8. Records first, memory second

28 September, 5 customers, 165 questions plus 10 disagreements
Summary

In a real bank the assistant is not working from memory alone. It has the account records: balances, cards, disputes, contact details. We built that records screen for each of the five customers, then re-asked the 165 questions with the records on screen and memory added beside them. We also wrote 10 questions where a stored note contradicts the record, to see which one the assistant follows.

Conclusion

Memory fills the gap the records leave, and the records keep memory honest. Records alone answered half the questions. Merged notes beside them fixed 78 of the 98 that need the conversation and cost only 1 of the 67 the records already covered, at a fraction of the cost of the whole history. A wrong note only wins where the bank has no record to contradict it.

Outside check

A published test, for scale

Ten questions from an academic memory benchmark

26 September, 10 of the 500 questions
Summary

To see whether our findings are peculiar to banking, we ran Memory Bank on ten questions from a public test of long-term chat memory. Each question hides its answer inside up to 48 earlier chat sessions.

Snapshot
Two things the user said in earlier sessions, weeks apart
USER: I've been working on an abstract ocean sculpture at home, and I've spent around 5-6 hours on it so far.

USER (later): I'm thinking of creating a sculpture inspired by the sunset. […] By the way, I've been spending a lot of time on my abstract ocean sculpture lately - I've already put in 10-12 hours, and it's still a work in progress.
The question

"How many hours have I spent on my abstract ocean sculpture?"

Expected answer: 10 to 12 hours. The test is whether memory keeps the newer figure rather than the older one. Memory Bank got this one right in every set-up.

Conclusion

Same failures as in our banking data. Facts the assistant said were never stored, dates were dropped so ordering and counting failed, a detail was reworded until it lost its meaning, and once the search found the wrong person with the same first name. The score is too small a sample to trust; the failure types are the finding.

Overall

What the six days add up to

  1. Give the assistant a memory. Without one it found the customer's real problem 11% of the time. With Memory Bank's search, 97%.
  2. Memory Bank's search is dependable, even on vague customer wording. Its note-writing is what needs care: it keeps what the customer said and drops a share of what the agent said, along with phone numbers and email addresses.
  3. On long, messy conversations, use the merged notes. They came within two answers of re-reading everything, at a twentieth of the reading, and 18 answers ahead of searching transcripts.
  4. Put the records first. Beside the bank's records, merged notes fixed 78 of 98 conversation questions and cost 1 of 67 record questions. A wrong note only wins where the records are silent.
  5. Expect two or three wrong notes per customer. Most are the customer's own misstatements stored as fact, they rarely reach an answer, and there is no setting to prevent them. A check against the records at write time would.
  6. Three weak spots remain. The briefing before a contact needs a wider look-up or a standing summary. Facts the agent said are stored less reliably than what the customer said. Dates get dropped.

Still open. Google's new structured customer profile feature is untested and is the obvious candidate for the briefing problem. A comparison with a rival memory service is planned but parked. Questions sent to Google about how notes are written and read are unanswered. The public benchmark should be run on more than ten questions before quoting a score.
